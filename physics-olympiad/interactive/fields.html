Practice

1. Charges +q and +4q are 3 m apart. Where is E = 0?

Between them, at distance x from +q: q/x² = 4q/(3 − x)², so 3 − x = 2x and x = 1 m. Load "q and 4q" and put the probe there: |E| drops to zero and field lines bend away (a saddle point of V).

2. A charge q sits at height d above a grounded plane. What is the force on it, and how much work does it take to pull it to infinity?

The image −q is at depth d, so F = kq²/(2d)², attractive. Work = ∫d∞ kq²/(4z²) dz = kq²/(4d). That is half of kq²/(2d), the energy of two real charges 2d apart. The image moves as you move q, and the field exists only above the plane.

3. Grounded sphere, radius R, charge q at distance D from the center. Find the image and check that V = 0 on the sphere.

q' = −qR/D at distance R²/D from the center. A point on the sphere is at distance r₁ from q and r₂ from q'. The triangles are similar, so r₂/r₁ = R/D, and kq/r₁ + kq'/r₂ = kq/r₁ − kq(R/D)/(r₁R/D) = 0 ✓. Load "Image: sphere": the green V = 0 contour lies on the dashed circle.

4. Why can't two field lines cross, and why do some lines from +2 in "+2 and −1" go off to infinity?

E has one direction at each point, so lines can't cross (except where E = 0). The net charge is +1, so at large distance the field looks like a single +1 charge. Of the lines leaving +2, only half end on −1. The rest escape.

Model: point charges in 3D, drawn in the plane that contains them. V = Σ kq/r and E = Σ kq r̂/r², with k = 1. The line density is only qualitative in a 2D slice of a 3D field, but the line directions and the equipotentials are exact.
